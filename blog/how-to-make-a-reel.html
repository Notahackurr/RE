How to make a reel: the six steps that actually matter

By J.P. Gentry · 18 November 2026 · 8 min read

The reel itself is not usually hard to film. The editing evening is the part that can turn one useful idea into a pile of footage you never post. A simple process helps you get from what you want to say to a finished reel without adding work that does not help.

Quick answer

  1. Choose one idea you can explain in a sentence.
  2. Film vertically, at eye level, with clear light and sound.
  3. Remove pauses and the run-up to your point.
  4. Add readable captions and highlight one word per line.
  5. Use cutaway footage only when it helps explain something.
  6. Post with a caption whose first three words give people a reason to keep reading.

1. Pick one idea worth saying out loud

Before you set up your phone, write one sentence that says what the viewer gets from the reel. Make it specific enough that someone can tell whether it's for them.

For example: “Here’s how I plan a week of client posts in 20 minutes.” That gives you a clear subject and a promise. “Some thoughts about content” does not give you much to build around.

If you can't say the point in one sentence, you've got more than one reel. Split the ideas instead of squeezing them into one take.

2. Film it in one take, vertically, at eye level

Turn your phone upright before you record. Frame yourself for a vertical video, with the camera close to eye level. A stack of books or a stable phone stand can help keep the shot still.

Face a window if you can, so the light falls towards you rather than coming from behind. Put your phone or microphone close enough to pick up your voice clearly. Clear sound matters more than a complicated background.

Speak through the idea in one take, even if you stumble. If you flub a line, pause, clap once, then say the line again. That gives you a clear marker to find when you edit. You can also stop and restart without a clap, but the clap makes the retake easier to spot.

Keep the delivery conversational. You're talking to one person, not giving a speech to a room. If a sentence feels awkward to say, rewrite it in words you'd actually use.

3. Cut the dead air first

Start by removing the pauses that make the reel feel slower than you actually talk. Look for long gaps, repeated starts, and the run-up before your point. Don't cut every breath. The aim is a natural pace, not a rushed voice.

Watch the full reel after you make the cuts. A sentence can sound fine on its own but feel abrupt when the line before it disappears. Keep enough space for the viewer to follow the thought.

This is often where a quick video turns into a long editing session. A useful limit is to make clarity edits first. Skip elaborate transitions until the reel makes sense from beginning to end.

4. Caption it so people can follow along

On-screen captions help make spoken words easier to follow, including when someone is watching without sound. Keep them readable on a phone, and check that they do not cover your face or the thing you are showing.

Break the words into short lines. Highlight one word per line so the viewer’s eye has a clear place to land. Use the highlight to draw attention to a key word, not to decorate every line.

Read through the captions once they're made. Fix names and any words the captioning tool got wrong. Good automatic captions can still miss an individual word.

5. Add footage or photos only where they clarify a line

A cutaway can make an idea easier to understand. If you say, “This is the setting I changed,” show the setting. If you mention a product, place or step, a relevant clip or photo can help the viewer see what you mean.

But don't add footage just because a section feels visually quiet. If the cutaway doesn't explain, prove or show a line, it may pull attention away from what you're saying. Your face and voice can carry a straight talking-to-camera reel on their own.

If you're filming extra clips, think about the sentence each one supports. That's more useful than filming a big folder of unrelated shots and forcing them in later.

6. Post it with a caption that earns the second watch

The video’s on-screen captions and the caption you write for the post are different things. Use the post caption to add context, name the takeaway or invite a useful next step. Avoid repeating the whole reel word for word.

Give the first three words a job. Start with the main idea, a clear benefit or the specific problem the reel addresses. For example: “Plan posts faster” tells people what the post is about before they read further. That's a writing choice, not a promise anyone will see it.

Before posting, check that the reel plays as intended, the captions are readable and the opening line makes sense without extra context. Choose a cover that clearly represents the topic. Then publish it where the people you want to reach can find it.

What to do when your reel looks bad

The sound is bad. Move closer to the phone or microphone and record somewhere quieter. If the room is noisy or your voice is hard to hear, fixing the sound may matter more than adding visuals.

The take feels flat. Check the first sentence. If it takes too long to reach the point, begin closer to the useful part. You can also record that line again with more energy, then use it as the opening.

The captions fight the footage. Shorten the lines, make sure the text is readable and move it away from the face or the detail you are showing. If the words are competing with a cutaway, keep the shot simpler.

The reel runs long. Look for repeated explanations, side points and pauses. Keep the lines that deliver the promise you made at the start. If you have two useful points, they may work better as two separate reels.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a reel be?

Make it long enough to explain one useful idea and no longer. If you need to cover several points, split them into separate reels. For long-form source videos, Reel Studio can make standalone reels from 15 to 90 seconds.

Do I need a ring light?

No. Try facing a window, and check your face is easy to see before you hit record. Clear sound and a steady frame matter more.

Can I make a reel from a podcast clip?

Yes. A podcast segment works as a reel if it makes sense on its own, without the full episode around it. Pick a section with a clear opening and a clean finish, then check the framing and captions before you post. Reel Studio's Long form to Reels turns a podcast into a batch of standalone clips.

How do I add captions to a reel?

You can add captions with the platform’s tools or use an editor that creates them from your speech. Either way, review the words, break them into readable lines and make sure they do not cover important parts of the video. Reel Studio creates English word-highlighted captions on-device with Whisper.

Is it worth filming in 4K for reels?

You don't need 4K for this. Reel Studio exports vertical video at 1080 × 1920. For most beginner reels, a clear, well-framed take with sound you can understand will do more than a higher resolution.
