Video editing for beginners: what to learn first and what to skip

By J.P. Gentry · 21 November 2026 · 6 min read

If you’re new to video editing, it’s easy to think you need to learn a whole editing program before you can post a reel. You don’t. If you’re speaking to camera, start with three things: remove the pauses that slow you down, add readable captions, and use a cutaway only when it helps explain your point.

This guide is for talking-to-camera short-form video. Editing a montage with no talking, or making frame-level changes across a full timeline, is a different skill. This isn’t a shortcut to mastering that kind of editing. It’s a simple way to get a clear talking video ready to post.

What editing a short-form video actually means

For a talking-to-camera reel, editing is mostly deciding what a viewer needs to hear and see, then removing what gets in the way. That might mean cutting a long gap, trimming the start of a sentence, or showing a detail while you explain it.

You don’t need to make every moment look different. The point is to make your idea easy to follow, not to prove you know the software. If the video makes sense and sounds natural, you’ve done the important work.

A simple first edit can follow this order:

  1. Watch the take once and decide what the main point is.
  2. Remove long pauses, flubs and the run-up to the useful part.
  3. Add captions and check that they match what you said.
  4. Add a cutaway only if it makes a line clearer.
  5. Watch the result once more before exporting.

The first edit is to cut dead air, not every breath

Start by looking for the gaps that make your delivery feel slower than it sounds in person. That can include a pause before you begin, a long search for the next thought, or a false start before the line you meant to say.

When you remove pauses from video, don’t try to erase every breath. Some space helps a viewer follow your thought. Keep a pause if it adds emphasis, gives a joke room to land, or makes an important point feel considered.

Try this on your first pass:

  • Cut the run-up if you take a while to start the useful sentence.
  • Remove repeated starts and clear flubs.
  • Shorten gaps that feel empty when you watch the whole take.
  • Keep the pause if it helps the meaning or delivery.

After a cut, listen to the sentence before and after it. If the words seem jammed together or the thought no longer makes sense, restore a little breathing room. A natural pace is more important than a fast one.

Add auto captions, then check them

Captions help viewers follow what you’re saying, including when they’re watching without sound. Keep them easy to read on a phone: use clear contrast, short lines and a position that doesn’t cover your face or anything you’re showing.

Auto captions can save you from typing every word, but they still need a quick review. Check names, numbers and any word the caption tool might have misheard. A small mistake in a person’s name or a key term can change the meaning of your line.

Don’t spend your first session testing every caption animation. A plain, readable style is enough to start. The words should support what you say, not compete with it.

Use a cutaway only when it clarifies the line

A cutaway is another shot, image or visual detail shown while your voice continues. It can help when you refer to something specific: a setting you’re explaining, a product you’re reviewing, or a step you want people to see.

Before adding one, ask what it helps the viewer understand. If the answer is “nothing in particular,” leave it out. Your face and voice can carry a talking-to-camera reel on their own.

A cutaway can also cover a jump cut if the switch in framing feels distracting. But you don’t need to hide every cut. For a straightforward video, a clear edit between sentences can be less distracting than an unrelated clip added for decoration.

Skip these editing skills at the start

These skills can be useful in the right project. They just aren’t the first things you need for a simple talking-head reel.

  • Transitions: A clean cut is usually enough when one thought follows another.
  • Colour grading: Start with a clear, well-lit recording. Detailed colour work can wait.
  • Fancy sound design: Make sure your voice is clear before adding sound effects.
  • Keyframing: This lets you animate changes over time, but it isn’t needed to trim a pause or add captions.

If you later want to edit montages, layer several kinds of footage, or make precise adjustments across a timeline, learning a full editor makes sense. That’s a separate learning path. For a talking take, you can publish a clear reel without tackling it first.

How long should a beginner edit take?

There isn’t one honest time estimate that fits every video. A clean short take with one clear idea is different from a recording with several retakes, lots of side points or hard-to-hear audio.

For now, aim to make one clarity pass, add captions, and review the finished video. If you find yourself adjusting tiny details before the message is clear, go back to the basics. Decide what the viewer needs, remove what slows them down, and stop when the video makes sense.

Frequently asked questions

What should I learn first in video editing?

For talking-to-camera short-form video, learn to remove unnecessary pauses, add readable captions and use cutaways only when they help explain a line. Those basics matter more than transitions or advanced effects.

How do I remove pauses from a video?

Review the whole take and shorten the gaps that feel empty, along with false starts and long run-ups. Keep pauses that help the delivery feel natural or let a point land. Watch across each cut to make sure the words still flow.

Are auto captions accurate enough to publish?

They’re a useful starting point, but check them before you post. Pay particular attention to names, numbers and terms that could be mistaken for another word.
