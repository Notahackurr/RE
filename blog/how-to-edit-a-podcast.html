How to edit a podcast: fix the spoken edit first, then pull the clips out

By J.P. Gentry · 23 November 2026 · 8 min read

A podcast episode can take hours to record and still have a lot of work left before it is ready to publish. Then, once it is out, the original file often sits untouched, even though it may hold several useful moments for short-form video.

The fix is to treat these as two connected jobs: edit the conversation so the episode flows, then find moments that can work on their own. The order matters. It is easier to choose good clips when you already know what the episode is really saying.

Podcast audio and video are two different edits

The audio edit is about what a listener hears. You remove mistakes and sections that slow the conversation, keep the meaning intact, and make sure the episode sounds consistent enough to follow.

The video edit is about what a viewer sees as well as hears. It may involve choosing a section of the recording, shaping it into a short clip, reframing it for a phone screen and adding captions.

People often call both jobs “editing the podcast,” but they are not the same. A clean video does not fix uneven audio levels, and a polished audio episode does not automatically make a good short.

If you record audio only, you can still publish the episode, but you need video footage before you can make video podcast clips. The footage might be a video recording of the conversation or a suitable on-camera segment. An audio file on its own cannot provide the person-on-camera video those clips need.

Cut the spoken content before polishing it

Start by listening for places where the conversation loses its thread. You are not trying to make people sound scripted. You are trying to help the listener stay with the point.

A transcript can make it easier to spot repeated starts and long detours. But listen to the audio around each cut before removing it. A line can look unnecessary on a page and still make the conversation clearer when spoken.

Here are made-up examples. They are not quotes from real shows.

Dead air and pauses

Before: “The first thing I’d change is the opening… [long pause] …because people need to know what the episode is about.”

After: “The first thing I’d change is the opening, because people need to know what the episode is about.”

Shorten a gap that feels empty, but keep a pause if it gives a thought room to land. Removing every breath can make a relaxed host sound rushed.

The false start

Before: “You should send the outline on Friday. Actually, no, send, send the finished version on Friday.”

After: “Send the finished version on Friday.”

Keep the correction if it adds meaning. If it is just the speaker finding the sentence, one clean version is usually enough.

The tangent that goes nowhere

Before: “The launch went well. We got good feedback. That reminds me of the first office I worked in, which had a tiny kitchen and this very strange coffee machine…” Then, several minutes later, back to the launch.

After: “The launch went well. We got good feedback. The main thing we learned was to send the welcome email sooner.”

Keep a side story if it gives the main point useful colour. Cut it if it pulls the listener away and never brings them back.

The promise to return

Before: “We’ll come back to pricing in a minute. First, let’s talk about how onboarding works.” The conversation moves on, and pricing never comes up again.

After: Remove the promise, or restore the pricing section if it belongs there. A listener may wait for a point you never return to.

Aim for clear sound, not broadcast perfection

A talking show does not need broadcast-grade audio to be worth listening to. Start with the problems that make speech hard to follow: a voice that is too quiet, distracting background noise, or one speaker much louder than another.

Then stop when the episode is comfortable to listen to. You do not need to remove every breath, polish every mouth sound or make each sentence sound studio-perfect. Over-editing can drain the warmth from a real conversation.

There is an important limit here: cutting video pauses is not the same as balancing audio levels or cleaning room echo. If those are problems in your recording, address them separately with an audio workflow or record in a quieter space next time.

Look for moments that stand on their own

Your episode is not the only thing in the file. A conversation may contain short sections that make sense without asking someone to listen to the full hour first.

Look for a question answered in one breath, a clear claim, a story with its own setup and payoff, or a useful number explained in context. A clip should leave a viewer with a complete thought, not just a sentence that sounds interesting when pulled out of the conversation.

Try this test: show someone the beginning of the clip without explaining the episode. Can they tell what the speaker is talking about? Does the moment reach an answer, example or conclusion? If not, include a little more setup or choose a different section.

Podcast clips often need less context than you think, but they still need enough. A bold line can catch attention; the rest of the clip has to deliver on what it suggests.

Put the strongest line at the start

The best hook may come halfway through a conversation. That is fine. A short clip does not need to follow the episode’s timeline from the beginning.

Suppose a guest explains a problem, gives a few details and then says, “The mistake was sending the welcome email three days late.” That last line might be the clearest opening. Put it first if it accurately represents the conversation, then include the context that makes it understandable.

Do not turn a careful point into a stronger claim than the speaker made. The hook should make the clip easier to enter, not change what the speaker meant. For more on the separate craft of a short-form post, see how to make a reel.

Use a one-hour editing block wisely

One hour will not always be enough to fully edit a 60-minute episode, especially if the recording has sound problems. Treat the hour as a focused work block, not a promise that every episode will be finished in that time. A transcript, recording notes or marked timestamps can help you spend less time searching.

  1. Set the goal. Decide what the full episode needs before it is publishable. Keep the original recording untouched so you can restore a cut.
  2. Make the big spoken cuts. Remove clear false starts, dead air and tangents that do not serve the episode. Do these before spending time tightening individual words.
  3. Check the edited conversation. Listen around your cuts and make sure they still sound natural. Restore a little space if the flow feels clipped.
  4. Mark clip candidates. Note sections with a strong claim, useful answer, story or number. A transcript can help you find the passages; listen to each one in context.
  5. Build the clips. Start with the strongest line that makes sense, include enough setup and stop after the payoff. Check that the clip ends cleanly.
  6. Review before you publish. Make sure captions match the speech, the hook does not misrepresent the point, and the video framing works for a phone screen.

If there are too many candidates to review carefully, choose fewer. A handful of complete moments is more useful than a long batch of clips that need fixing.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to edit a podcast?

It depends on the recording, the sound quality and how much you cut. A focused hour can be enough to make meaningful progress, but it may not be enough to fully edit a 60-minute episode and create clips. Transcript notes and timestamps can help you use your editing time well.

Do I need to edit the audio before making clips?

If you plan to publish the full episode, make the important spoken-content cuts first. That gives you a clearer version to pull clips from. Audio levels and room echo are separate from cutting pauses and selecting moments, so deal with those separately if needed.

How many clips can I get from one episode?

There is no set number. It depends on how many complete, useful moments the conversation contains. Look for a clear opening, a thought that stands on its own and a satisfying finish rather than trying to hit a target count.

Can I make shorts from an audio-only podcast?

Not directly as video clips. You need video footage of a person speaking on camera, or suitable video footage to pair with the audio. Once you have video, you can select moments from the conversation and shape them for short-form platforms.

What makes a good podcast clip?

A good clip gives a new viewer enough context to follow the point, then delivers an answer, story, example or takeaway. Start with a clear line, but keep it true to what the speaker said.
