AI b-roll: what it does well, and when a real clip is the better cutaway

By J.P. Gentry · 24 November 2026 · 8 min read

You finish a take, watch it back, and your eye starts drifting. Every eight or ten seconds you want something else to look at. So you search for a way to fill those gaps and the answer that comes back is AI b-roll: a tool that invents footage for you.

It works sometimes. It is also the wrong default for most talking-head reels, and the reason is not that the footage looks fake. It is that invented footage cannot show your thing.

What b-roll is actually for

A cutaway does one of three jobs.

  • It covers a cut, so a trimmed pause or a jump between takes does not feel like a glitch.
  • It shows the thing you are describing, so the viewer sees it instead of assembling it in their head.
  • It gives the eye a rest, so the next shot of your face lands harder.

Only the second job needs new footage. The first is a technical fix and the third is pacing. If you go looking for AI b-roll every time any of those three comes up, you end up covering a reel that did not need covering.

What AI b-roll does well

Generated footage earns its place when the shot could not exist otherwise.

If you are talking about an idea rather than an object, there is nothing to film. An empty stadium at dusk for a line about chasing followers. A slow push through clouds for a line about waiting. A crowded street for a line about noise. You cannot shoot those on a Tuesday afternoon in your kitchen, and stock libraries are full of the same six versions everyone else already used.

Generated shots are also consistent in a way stock is not. You can hold one look across a whole set of reels, where stock footage jumps between cameras, colour grades and decades.

And a good generated shot beats a bad stock clip. If the alternative is a clip of a person pointing at a laptop that has nothing to do with your line, generated footage at least matches the mood. Both of those still beat a cutaway that quietly lies about what you are saying.

Where AI b-roll falls apart on a talking-head reel

It cannot show your specific thing

Specificity is what makes a reel feel real. "My dog ate the invoice" is a good line because it is your dog and your invoice. A generated dog is a dog. Your phone photo of the shredded corner is the shot, and it takes four seconds to add.

The more personal, local or particular the line, the worse a generated cutaway fits. That covers most of what solo creators actually talk about: their work, their clients, their kitchen, their week.

Generated footage drifts

Hands get an extra finger or lose one. Text on a screen becomes a language that does not exist. Objects change shape between frames. Reflections and shadows disagree with the light.

You will notice all of it at 1080 by 1920 on a phone, because that is where it will mostly be watched, and vertical frames crop in tight rather than hide detail. A shot that survives a laptop preview can fall apart at full screen on a handset.

You have to check the terms yourself

Every generative video tool writes its own rules about commercial use, and they change. Some grant you the output outright, some restrict certain uses, some say nothing clear at all.

Read the terms of whichever tool you use before you build a reel on top of its footage. Do not take a blog post's summary of them, including mine.

It spends the thing you are short of

Prompting, re-rolling, discarding, prompting again. The generation is fast; the choosing is not. Most of the time goes into deciding which of the four attempts is usable, and then into deciding whether "usable" is actually good enough.

You started editing to save an evening. Generating cutaways can spend it.

The real problem is matching, not generating

Here is the thing worth sitting with: most creators do not have a footage shortage. They have a matching problem.

The hard part of b-roll is not obtaining a clip. It is answering two questions about each line of your take. Does this line earn a cutaway at all? And if it does, what does the line actually show?

Answer those two and the source of the clip becomes a small decision. Your library, a stock search, a generated shot, or nothing. Answer them badly and no amount of generated footage saves the reel, because you will have filled every gap with something that does not belong there.

This is why "AI b-roll generator" is the wrong starting point. It is a supplier, not an editor.

A simple order of operations

Work through these in order for each line that feels like it needs a cutaway.

  1. Does the line name something you already have? An object, a place, a person, a screen, a document. Use your own clip or photo. It is the most specific option and usually the fastest.
  2. Is the line abstract? A feeling, a comparison, a number, a period of time. Then reach for a concept shot, either stock or generated, and pick the one that matches the mood of the sentence rather than the literal noun.
  3. Does the line need nothing at all? Leave your face on screen. A reel is not improved by covering it.

Placement rules that keep a cutaway from breaking the reel

  • Never cut away from the opening line. The hook has to be your face and your words, or the first two seconds read as an ad.
  • Do not cut away during the sentence you want the viewer to remember.
  • Cut on the word that names the thing, not on a random beat.
  • Hold the cutaway just long enough to register, then come back before the next sentence lands.
  • One idea per cutaway. Two concepts in one shot reads as decorative.
  • If you cannot say what the cutaway adds in one sentence, cut it.

How to check a generated shot before you use it

Watch it muted, at arm's length, on the smallest screen you own. That is roughly the conditions your viewer is in, and flaws you have stopped seeing after the third review become obvious again at thumbnail size.

Then pause on individual frames and check hands, text, reflections and shadows. Those four are where generation gives itself away.

Last check: show it to someone without saying it was generated. If they ask how you filmed it, it is fine. If they ask what is wrong with it, you have your answer.

Frequently asked questions

Is AI b-roll allowed on Instagram Reels and TikTok?

Both platforms ask you to label realistic AI-generated media, and their rules on what counts keep moving. Check the current guidance on the platform you post to, and label generated footage if it could be mistaken for a real event. Your own filmed footage and licensed stock have no such question attached.

Can I use AI b-roll for a client's video?

Only if the tool's terms allow commercial use and your client is fine with generated footage. Many are not, especially in health, finance and anything where a shot could imply a real product, place or person. Ask before you make it, not after.

Do I need b-roll in every reel?

No. A tight sixty-second take with good captions and a clear point can hold attention on its own, and a reel that keeps the speaker on screen the whole time has nothing between the viewer and the point. Cover the cuts and the moments where the viewer needs to see the thing. Leave the rest.

What is the difference between b-roll and a cutaway?

People use them interchangeably, and close enough. B-roll is the wider term: any supporting footage laid over your main shot. A cutaway is the specific move of leaving the speaker to show it. So a cutaway is always b-roll, but b-roll under a voiceover you recorded separately never cuts away from anyone.

Which is better, AI b-roll or stock footage?

It depends on the line. Generated wins for concepts and moods you cannot film, and for a consistent look across a set. Stock wins for real places, real objects and anything a viewer might recognise, and it is usually faster to find. Your own footage wins over both whenever it is specific to what you are saying.
